The 1×1 Motion

How to sell antiloki one conversation at a time — who is worth a call, what to ask before you demo anything, what to show and in which order, and what to send after. Built on the kit's positioning, pricing and three provable claims. Nothing here contradicts it.

$200 / yr $500 lifetime $800 5 seats trial 7 days blended ARPU $330

01The decision this whole page rests on

A 1×1 motion is expensive — roughly 45 minutes of your life per live conversation once you count research, the thread, the call and the follow-up. So the first question is not "how do I sell 1×1", it is "which conversations are worth having at all". The pricing answers it for you.

$20
value of a call to close one $200 yearly licence at 10%
$125
value of a call to close one $500 lifetime at 25%
$200+
value of a call to close 5 seats at $800 at 25%
So: 1×1 selling is a team motion. Individual buyers are a self-serve funnel — trial, sequence, checkout — and putting a call in front of a $200 licence destroys the economics of your week. Your calendar belongs to people who can put seats on a card. Everyone else gets a key, not a meeting.

That gives you two tracks, with different goals, different scripts and different definitions of success. Run both — they feed each other.

Track A · Seeding segments 1 & 3

Power users and agency folk. You are not trying to close them.

  • Goal: 30 lifetime keys given away in month 1.
  • Ask for: one line of honest feedback, and permission to quote it.
  • Win looks like: 5 usable quotes, 3 clips someone else recorded, and referrals into Track B.
  • Never: book a 30-minute call for this. It happens in DMs.

Track B · Closing segment 2

Tech leads with 3–10 devs and a budget line. This is where the money is.

  • Goal: one team deal a week by week 6.
  • Path: trigger → DM → 20-min call → 7-day pilot → readout → invoice.
  • Win looks like: $800–$3,200 invoiced with VAT details, and a named champion inside.
  • Never: demo before discovery. Never discount the yearly.
The bridge between them is one question. Every person you hand a key to in Track A works somewhere. At the end of the thread, ask: "who else on your team runs more than one agent at a time?" That single sentence converts a free key into a qualified Track B lead, and it is the highest-leverage thing in this document.

02Who gets a call

A named human, with a trigger, who can spend $800 without a committee. All three, or it is not a Track B lead — it is a Track A key.

Where the triggers actually are — 30 minutes a day, 10 new names

  • X search, daily: "claude code" (parallel OR worktree OR "two sessions" OR overwrote OR cost) — reply usefully in public first, DM only people who engaged back.
  • LinkedIn: title is Engineering Manager / Tech Lead / Staff Engineer and they posted in the last 30 days about Claude Code, Codex, Cursor or agents. The post is your opening line.
  • Reddit: r/ClaudeAI and r/ChatGPTCoding — answer the question properly, in public, with a number. The DM comes later and only to repliers.
  • Discord (Claude, OpenCode, Cursor): answer three questions a day with the product unmentioned. Keep a list of who asked good questions — that list is worth more than any scrape.
  • Your own GitHub: anyone who opened an issue or starred the repo has already raised their hand. Highest reply rate you will get.
Do not buy a list. Every number in section 06 assumes a real trigger in the first line. Cold-blast outreach converts at roughly a tenth of this and burns the accounts you need for the next 90 days.

03The call · 20 minutes, in four movements

Twenty minutes, not thirty. A short meeting gets accepted, forces you to cut, and ends before the energy drops. The order below is the whole method: you do not show anything until they have told you what hurts.

0–2 min
Frame it and give them the exitSay how long it will take and that "this isn't for you" is a fine outcome. It lowers the defence more than any rapport-building does.
2–8 min
Discovery — five questions, and you shut upYou are listening for two things: the collision story, and who signs. If you leave without both, the call was a demo, not a sale.
8–17 min
Demo only what they namedThree beats, each tied to something they just said. A feature they did not complain about does not get shown.
17–20 min
One ask, with a date on itNever "I'll send some info". A pilot with two names, one repo and a readout already in the calendar.

The five questions

discovery · ask in this order, do not lead the witness
1. On a normal day, how many agent sessions are running at once across the team?

2. Tell me about the last time two of them collided. What actually happened?

3. When someone asks "what did the agent change last sprint" — where do you look?

4. Do you know what you spent on agents last month? Per repo, or per person?

5. If you wanted this for the team, is that your call or does it go to someone else?

Q2 is the one that sells. Let the silence sit after it — people talk themselves into the problem far better than you can. Q5 is the one everyone skips and then wonders why the deal stalls in week three.

Say the token number out loud. "Faster and verified, for roughly twice the tokens" is the most persuasive sentence in the demo, because nobody selling AI tooling volunteers the cost side. It buys you the benefit of the doubt on everything else you claim.
Never in a call: "10× faster" · "autonomous" · "replaces your engineers" · "AI-powered". Say parallel, scoped, verified, on the record, on your machine. And claim only the three things the kit says you can prove — if they ask for a fourth, say you have not measured it yet and offer to.

04Objections, answered live

Each of these is a real position, not a brush-off. Answer the strongest version of it, then hand back control with a question.

They sayYou say
"We already use worktrees."Good — then you are halfway. A worktree isolates a checkout; it does not stop a write. The agent can still edit anything inside its tree, and you find out at diff time. This refuses the write at the tool call. Want me to show the refusal on a folder you pick?
"Isn't this a wrapper around Claude Code?"It is a control layer over the CLIs you already pay for, and deliberately so — we do not want to be your model vendor. You keep your subscriptions; we box them, verify them and record them. Which CLIs are you on today?
"What leaves our machines?"Three things, and this is the whole list: your CLIs' own calls to their providers, exactly as today; a daily licence check, a key and a machine id; an update check against a static file on GitHub. No account, no telemetry, state sits in .antiloki/ next to the code. Do you need that in writing for security review?
"It's too expensive."Against what? Five seats is $800 a year — less than one Max seat. You are already spending more than that on the agents themselves; this is the layer that tells you where it went. What did the team spend on agents last month?
"Anthropic or OpenAI will just build this."One of them might build it for their own CLI. Neither will build the one that runs their competitor's agent next to theirs on your laptop, and that is the whole point of a desk. How many vendors are you running right now?
"No budget this quarter."Then do not buy anything this quarter. Trial is seven days and free — run it on one real ticket, keep the timeline, and put it in the budget conversation with evidence. When does the next budget cycle open?
"Send me something and I'll look."I will, but the written version is the weakest version of this — the refusal has to be watched. Fifteen minutes on Thursday and I will run it on a repo shaped like yours?

05The pilot — what actually closes a team

Nobody buys seats off a demo. They buy off their own timeline, from their own repo, with their own ticket in it. The pilot exists to manufacture that artifact, and it has to be designed or it drifts into a free trial nobody finishes.

Four conditions, agreed on the call

  1. Two or three named devs. Not "the team" — names. Anything broader and nobody owns it.
  2. One real repo and one real ticket. A sandbox proves nothing and everyone knows it.
  3. A written success criterion. Theirs, in their words, before it starts. Usually: "one goal run on a real ticket, verified, and a timeline we can paste into the PR."
  4. A readout in the calendar now. 15 minutes, day 7, invite sent before you hang up. Without the date the pilot dies quietly.

The readout, and the ask

Open their timeline, not your slides. Walk the run they did. Then:

That's your run, on your repo — 
the refusals, the verdicts, the cost.

Five seats is $800 for the year, 
invoiced with your VAT details. 

Shall I send it today, or is there 
someone who needs to see this first?

"Or is there someone who needs to see this first" surfaces the real approver without making them admit they are not it.

07The scripts

The kit's DM playbooks cover the opening. These are the ones it does not have — the moments where a 1×1 deal is actually won or lost.

Booking the call, after they describe the problem

track b · the ask that gets a yes
That's the exact pattern I built this for.

Rather than describe it: 15 minutes, I run it on a repo shaped like yours,
and you tell me where it breaks. If it's not useful you've lost a quarter
of an hour and I'll send the benchmark write-up anyway.

Thursday 10:00 or 16:00?

Two specific times, never "does some time work". Naming the exit ("if it's not useful") raises acceptance more than any benefit you could add.

The seeding DM — Track A, no call, no pitch

track a · after they engaged with something you said in public
Hey — you replied on the worktree/scope thread.

I package that setup as a local app: Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode side
by side, each boxed to the folders you allow, one timeline with the cost.

Want a lifetime key? No strings and nothing to cancel — I want one honest
line about what's wrong with it, and that's the whole deal.

The referral bridge — send this to every Track A key holder

the highest-leverage message in this document
Thanks — that's genuinely useful, I'll fix the {thing they flagged}.

One last thing and then I'll leave you alone: who else where you work runs
more than one agent at a time? The folder-box thing lands differently with
whoever has to answer "what did the AI change last sprint".

Happy to hand them a key too, or stay out of it entirely — your call.

"Or stay out of it entirely" is doing the work. It makes the intro voluntary, which is the only kind that ever arrives warm.

The stall — day 10, no reply after a pilot

the close-out. send it, then actually stop.
I think this one didn't land, which is fine — closing it on my side so

This is the highest-reply-rate message you will send. It gets answers because it asks for nothing and it is visibly the last one.

Three ways this motion dies. Demoing before discovery, so you show the wrong thing to someone who never told you what hurt. Taking calls for $200 licences, so your week fills with work worth $20 an hour. And letting pilots run without a readout date, so they decay into abandoned trials that no follow-up can rescue.
